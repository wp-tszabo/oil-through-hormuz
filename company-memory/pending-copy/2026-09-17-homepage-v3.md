## The one judgement call in this draft, stated plainly

Directive 2 sits next to `GOVERNANCE.md` critical-issue category 4 and
`rubric.md` §1.1/§3.2, which forbid overstating the data's authority or its
originality. Today the model has **exactly one** licence-clear numeric input
(EIA's public-domain quarterly series). Two extremes were both rejected:

- **Strip the attribution entirely** and let "our proprietary model" imply we
  observe the strait ourselves or aggregate many feeds. That is a category-4
  misrepresentation and a rubric FAIL. Not done.
- **Refuse the directive** and keep leading with the provider's name. Not
  warranted — nothing about moving a citation off the headline is dishonest,
  and the owner's product reasoning is sound.

**Synthesis shipped here:** the *visible, primary* copy speaks in terms of
"our model" and "our method". The *full* attribution — publisher named, dataset
named, release date, licence, the provenance caveat, and the list of sources
checked and rejected — moves intact to a linked **Sources & methodology** page,
reachable from the chart caption, from the methodology blurb, and from the
footer. Nothing false is claimed; nothing is hidden; every number on the page
is still one click from its citation.

The draft also does **not** claim more inputs than exist. It describes the
*aggregation approach* as the product's differentiator and as ongoing work —
which is true — and says in plain words on the Sources page that the model
currently runs on a single cleared dataset.

### Methodology blurb (the "appealing" rewrite)

> ## How we get to a number
>
> Nobody publishes a daily figure for the Strait of Hormuz that is free to
> republish. The public record arrives a quarter at a time, months after the
> fact. The trackers that watch the strait day by day are locked behind
> commercial licences.
>
> So we build our own. Our model takes the public evidence we can legally
> stand behind, calibrates against the quarterly record, and carries it
> forward to the day you are reading — with a range that reflects how much we
> genuinely know rather than how confident we would like to sound. Right now
> that range is wide: flows fell from 21.6 to 4.9 million barrels per day in
> three quarters, and our model knows it is estimating through a disruption.
> When flows settle, the range closes to around ±3%.
>
> This is our estimate, not an official measurement — and that is the product.
> The method is ours, it is published in full, and it is scored against every
> new figure that lands. Widening what feeds it — more public data, official
> reports, credible reporting — is continuous work, and the
> [sources and method](sources.html) page always says exactly what is in the
> model today.

## Page 2 — `site/sources.html` — "Sources & methodology"

Heading: **Sources & methodology**

> ### What the model is
>
> A proprietary estimate of how much crude oil, condensate and petroleum
> products are moving through the Strait of Hormuz on a given day, expressed
> in million barrels per day, built only from inputs we are legally permitted
> to republish.
>
> ### What is in it today
>
> One cleared dataset, and we say so rather than implying a wider net:
> the **U.S. Energy Information Administration**'s
> [Global Energy Security Data](https://www.eia.gov/outlooks/steo/report/energysecurity/article.php),
> Table 4, released **12 August 2026**, covering quarters through April–June
> 2026. It is a work of the U.S. federal government and is in the public
> domain. EIA's volumes are based on Vortexa tanker tracking data with
> additional EIA analysis; we republish EIA's published analysis and do not
> hold or license Vortexa's underlying data.
>
> EIA notes that since the end of February 2026, AIS signal data for ships
> transiting the strait has become especially unreliable, and that 2026 Hormuz
> volumes are being revised frequently as better information arrives. We treat
> the anchor as provisional for exactly that reason.
>
> ### What is deliberately not in it
>
> Several daily trackers exist. We have checked their terms and rejected them:
> IMF PortWatch and the WTO/AXSMarine Hormuz tracker are both closed to
> redistribution. We do not use an uncleared source "just to calibrate" — if
> it moves the number, it needs a licence.
>
> ### How the estimate is built
>
> The latest published quarterly average is the anchor. With no cleared
> higher-frequency input, we carry it forward unchanged rather than inventing
> a shape inside the quarter that we cannot observe — a smoother curve would
> be false precision, not better information.
>
> The range is not a guess about a guess. We back-tested the same method
> against the published record: predicting each quarter from the one before
> was accurate to within 1.4% while flows were steady, then overshot by 45%
> and by 204% as flows collapsed through 2026. A range wide enough to have
> contained that collapse runs from about 70% below the anchor to about 40%
> above it — today, 1.5 to 6.9 million barrels per day. A mechanical test
> decides which regime we are in: anything beyond ±10% quarter-on-quarter is
> "disrupted", and the range widens accordingly. Every time a new quarter
> publishes, the anchor resets, the previous estimate is scored against the
> actual, and that error joins the record the range is derived from.
>
> ### Where this is going
>
> The method is the product, and it is not finished. Expanding the set of
> inputs — additional public datasets, official reports, credible reporting —
> is standing, continuous work. Each addition has to clear the same bar: we
> must be legally permitted to use and republish it. When the set changes,
> this page changes with it.
>
> The full working method, including the back-test and every source checked
> and rejected on licensing grounds, is in the
> [public project repository](https://github.com/wp-tszabo/oil-through-hormuz/blob/main/company-memory/methodology.md).
