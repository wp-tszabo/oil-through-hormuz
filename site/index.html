Realistically, somewhere between 1.5 and 6.9 million barrels per day.

Nobody measures this strait daily in a way we are allowed to republish, so this figure is produced by our own model rather than observed. It carries forward the most recent measured value — an average of 4.9 million barrels per day for April–June 2026, published by the U.S. Energy Information Administration on 12 August 2026 — because no source we may use shows what has happened since. Treat the range as the answer and the single number as its midpoint.

The range is wide deliberately. Flows fell from 21.6 to 14.9 to 4.9 million barrels per day over three quarters, and back-testing this model across that collapse produced errors of +45% and +204%; across the calm quarters before it, the same model was accurate to within 1.4%. Our regime test currently reads disrupted, which is why the band is this wide. It narrows to about ±3% when flows steady.

Methodology

The headline figure is an estimate produced by us. It is not a measurement, and no government or official body stands behind it. Every source that tracks this strait daily is commercially licensed or otherwise closed to republication, so the fastest cadence legally available to us is a quarterly average, published roughly six weeks after the quarter ends. The estimate exists to bridge that gap honestly, not to disguise it.

How it is built: we take the most recent quarterly average EIA has actually measured and published — currently 4.9 million barrels per day for April–June 2026 — and carry it forward unchanged to the day being estimated. We do not smooth, curve-fit or interpolate, because we have no licensed higher-frequency input that would justify inventing a shape within the quarter; a more elaborate curve would be false precision rather than better information.

The range around it is not a guess about a guess. It comes from back-testing the same method against the six quarters EIA has published: predicting each quarter from the one before was accurate to within 1.4% while flows were steady, then overshot by 45% and by 204% as flows collapsed through 2026. A band wide enough to have contained that collapse runs from about 70% below the anchor to about 40% above it, which on today’s anchor is the 1.5–6.9 million barrels per day shown above. A mechanical test decides which regime we are in — flagged “disrupted” whenever the latest quarter-on-quarter change exceeds ±10% — and the band is set accordingly. Each time EIA publishes a new quarter, the estimate is re-anchored, the previous estimate is scored against the new actual, and that error is added to the record the band is derived from.

Two honest caveats. The anchor itself is provisional: EIA reports that AIS signal data for ships transiting the strait has been especially unreliable since the end of February 2026, and its 2026 figures have already been revised between releases. And the estimate cannot see inside the quarter it is anchored on — if flows moved sharply after June 2026, this page has no way of knowing, which is exactly what the range is there to tell you.

The full working method, including the sources we checked and rejected on licensing grounds and the back-test behind that range, is in the project repository, which is public.
